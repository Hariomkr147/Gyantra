# Chapter 8: Force and Laws of Motion

## 8.1 Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

A force can be a push or a pull. When several forces act on a body at the same
time, their effects can cancel one another. Such forces are called balanced
forces. If the net force on an object is not zero, the forces are unbalanced and
the object accelerates in the direction of the net force.

Consider a wooden block resting on a table. The weight of the block acts
downwards and the table pushes upwards with an equal force. These two forces are
balanced, so the block stays at rest. Now imagine pushing the block sideways. If
the applied force is greater than the force of friction, the block begins to
move. The forces are now unbalanced.

An unbalanced force is needed to change the state of motion of an object. It can
change the speed of the object, the direction of its motion, or both.

## 8.2 First Law of Motion

An object remains in a state of rest or of uniform motion in a straight line
unless acted upon by an unbalanced external force. This is Newton's first law of
motion, also called the law of inertia.

Inertia is the natural tendency of an object to resist a change in its state of
motion. The mass of an object is a measure of its inertia. A heavier object has
greater inertia than a lighter one, so a larger force is needed to change its
state of motion.

Everyday observations illustrate this law. When a moving bus brakes suddenly,
passengers lean forward because their bodies continue to move even though the bus
has slowed. When the bus starts suddenly, passengers fall backwards, because
their bodies were at rest and tend to remain at rest.

## 8.3 Second Law of Motion

The rate of change of momentum of an object is proportional to the applied
unbalanced force in the direction of the force.

Momentum is the product of the mass and the velocity of an object.

p = m * v

The second law can be written in the familiar form:

F = m * a

where F is the force applied, m is the mass of the object, and a is the
acceleration produced. The SI unit of force is the newton (N). One newton is the
force that produces an acceleration of 1 metre per second squared in an object of
mass 1 kilogram.

Example 8.1: A force of 5 N acts on a mass of 2 kg. The acceleration produced is
a = F/m = 5/2 = 2.5 metre per second squared.

Example 8.2: A cricket player pulls his hands backwards while catching a fast
ball. By doing so he increases the time taken to reduce the momentum of the ball
to zero. Since the rate of change of momentum is reduced, the force exerted on
his hands is smaller and injury is avoided.

## 8.4 Third Law of Motion

To every action there is an equal and opposite reaction. These forces always act
on two different bodies, never on the same body.

When a bullet is fired from a gun, the gun exerts a forward force on the bullet
and the bullet exerts an equal backward force on the gun. This is why the gun
recoils. Because the mass of the gun is much larger than that of the bullet, the
acceleration of the gun is much smaller.

A person walking pushes backwards on the ground, and the ground pushes the person
forward with an equal force. Without this reaction force, walking would not be
possible.

## 8.5 Conservation of Momentum

When two objects collide, the total momentum of the two objects before the
collision is equal to the total momentum after the collision, provided no
external unbalanced force acts on them. This is the law of conservation of
momentum.

| --- | --- | --- |
| First | An object keeps its state of rest or uniform motion unless an unbalanced force acts | Inertia |
| Second | Rate of change of momentum is proportional to the applied force | F equals m times a |
